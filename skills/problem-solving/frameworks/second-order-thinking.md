# Second-Order Thinking (Howard Marks)

## When this lens applies

Use when the operator is weighing a decision whose first-order effect is obvious
but whose downstream effects on customers, incentives, culture, competitors, or
cash are not. Especially valuable for pricing changes, hiring/firing, public
commitments, and policy changes that change *behavior*, not just outcomes.

## Prompt template

```
I'm about to make a decision and I want you to help me think through
it using Second-Order Thinking, as described by Howard Marks. Most
people only think about the immediate result of a decision. I want
you to map out the consequences in three layers:

First order: What happens immediately?
Second order: What does that cause to happen next?
Third order: What does THAT cause 6-12 months from now?

Do this for both the 'I do it' and 'I don't do it' paths. Be
brutally honest about the downstream effects.

My decision: <DECISION>
```

## What a strong answer looks like

- Both paths analyzed (act / don't act) -- asymmetric coverage defeats the lens.
- Each order is *causally* linked to the previous one -- not a list of three
  unrelated guesses at different timescales.
- 3rd-order effects touch behavior, not just numbers -- who changes what they do
  in response to the 2nd-order signal.
- Calls out at least one *non-obvious* downstream effect -- a 2nd or 3rd order
  consequence the operator hasn't already considered. If the whole map is
  obvious, the lens didn't earn its dispatch.
- Closes by naming which path has the better 3rd-order position, not just the
  better 1st-order outcome.

## Failure modes to avoid

- Stopping at first-order ("revenue goes up") -- that's not this lens, that's a
  spreadsheet.
- Three orders of the same effect at three sizes (small / medium / large) --
  that's a forecast, not causal chaining.
- Symmetric, generic downsides on both paths ("could be risky") -- the operator
  can write those themselves.
- Ignoring competitor / counterparty response. If the decision is visible to
  anyone outside the operator, their reaction is a 2nd-order effect.
